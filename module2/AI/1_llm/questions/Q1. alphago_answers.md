## Question 1.1

Why was Go considered "impossible" for AI for decades, while chess had already been solved by Deep Blue in 1997?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Go's board is 19×19 (361 intersections) vs chess's 8×8 (64 squares).
[*] Go has ~10¹⁷⁰ possible board states vs chess's ~10⁴⁷ — more than the number of atoms in the observable universe (~10⁸⁰).
[*] Go has ~250 average moves per turn vs ~35 in chess, making brute-force search impossible.
[*] Go positions are extremely hard to evaluate even for experts — it relies on intuition and pattern recognition that couldn't be captured in hand-crafted rules.
[ ] Go was impossible because AI computers lacked enough RAM to store the board.
[ ] Chess was easier because it uses fewer pieces on the board.

## Question 1.2

What was the "secret match" before the Lee Sedol match, and what happened to Fan Hui afterwards?

---

**Answer:**

[*] In October 2015, AlphaGo secretly played Fan Hui (European Go champion, 2-dan professional) and won 5–0.
[*] DeepMind kept the match secret for months while preparing to face Lee Sedol (9-dan professional).
[*] After losing, Fan Hui joined the DeepMind team as a consultant and spent months playing against AlphaGo.
[*] Fan Hui's world ranking improved significantly after training with AlphaGo — he said it helped him understand Go better.
[ ] Fan Hui retired from Go immediately after losing to AlphaGo.
[ ] The match was public and broadcast live worldwide.

## Question 1.3

What was "Move 37" in Game 2, and why was it historically significant?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Move 37 was a stone placed on the fifth line early in the game — a position every classical Go textbook for 2,000 years would call a bad move.
[*] Professional commentators initially thought it was a mistake.
[*] Dozens of moves later it became clear that Move 37 had set up a devastating winning strategy.
[*] Professional players called it "a move from the future" — a move that thousands of years of human Go study had never discovered.
[ ] Move 37 was the move where Lee Sedol resigned the game.
[ ] Move 37 placed a stone in the center of the board, which is standard Go theory.

## Question 1.4

What was "Move 78" in Game 4, and what does it represent?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Move 78 was a wedge move played by Lee Sedol that commentators initially dismissed but turned out to be brilliant.
[*] AlphaGo's response was confused and it began making clearly bad moves — it couldn't handle the unexpected situation.
[*] Lee won Game 4 — the only game a human ever won against AlphaGo in a formal match.
[*] Move 78 was later called "the most beautiful move ever played in Go" because it was a human finding a flaw in the machine.
[ ] Move 78 was played by AlphaGo, not Lee Sedol.
[ ] Lee Sedol won Games 4 and 5.

## Question 1.5

AlphaGo used two neural networks plus a search algorithm. What role did each component play?

---

**Answer:**

[*] **Policy Network** — trained on 30 million positions from expert human games; predicts probability of each possible move; acts like intuition, narrowing from 250 moves to a handful.
[*] **Value Network** — predicts the probability of winning from any given board position; acts like judgment.
[*] **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)** — uses both networks to simulate future games, exploring promising moves deeply and pruning bad ones.
[*] The combination: Policy/Value Network (deep learning intuition) + MCTS (calculation) = superhuman Go play.
[ ] AlphaGo used only brute-force search, just like Deep Blue.
[ ] The value network chose which moves to play; the policy network evaluated positions.

## Question 1.6

What is AlphaGo Zero, and how was it different from the original AlphaGo?

---

**Answer:**

[*] AlphaGo Zero (October 2017) learned from zero human games — purely self-play starting from random moves.
[*] Original AlphaGo learned from 30 million positions from expert human games, then improved by self-play.
[*] After just 3 days of training (4.9 million self-play games), AlphaGo Zero beat the version that defeated Lee Sedol — 100 games to 0.
[*] After 40 days, it became the strongest Go player in history.
[ ] AlphaGo Zero was slower than the original AlphaGo because it had no human data.
[ ] AlphaGo Zero required 10 years of self-play to surpass human-trained AlphaGo.

## Question 1.7

What did AlphaGo Zero discover about human Go knowledge (joseki)?

---

**Answer:**

[*] AlphaGo Zero independently rediscovered classic human joseki (定石) — established patterns humans developed over thousands of years.
[*] It then discovered entirely new strategies that humans had never seen.
[*] Some joseki considered "correct" for centuries were revealed to be suboptimal — AlphaGo Zero found better alternatives.
[*] Humans studied Go for 4,000 years; an AI starting from nothing surpassed all of that in 40 days.
[ ] AlphaGo Zero confirmed that all existing joseki were optimal.
[ ] AlphaGo Zero could only play known human patterns, not discover new ones.

## Question 1.8

What is AlphaZero, and what did it demonstrate about general AI learning?

---

**Answer:**

[*] AlphaZero used the exact same algorithm as AlphaGo Zero — no human data, pure self-play — for three different games.
[*] Results: Go (34 hours) → beat AlphaGo Zero; Chess (9 hours) → beat Stockfish; Shogi (12 hours) → beat Elmo.
[*] One algorithm, three games, superhuman in all three — demonstrating general-purpose AI learning.
[*] No human knowledge was required in any of the three games.
[ ] AlphaZero required separate algorithms for each of the three games.
[ ] AlphaZero used human game data for chess and shogi but not for Go.

## Question 1.9

What was AlphaFold, and what was its significance for science?

---

**Answer:**

[*] AlphaFold solved the protein folding problem — predicting a protein's 3D structure from its amino acid sequence, an unsolved problem for 50 years.
[*] At the CASP14 competition in 2020, AlphaFold 2 achieved a median score of 92.4/100, vs ~70 for the next best team; scores above 90 are considered experimentally accurate.
[*] In 2022, DeepMind released predicted structures for over 200 million proteins — nearly every known protein — for free.
[*] What used to take a PhD student years of lab work, AlphaFold predicts in minutes.
[ ] AlphaFold was a chess-playing AI that was later adapted for biology.
[ ] AlphaFold's results were considered preliminary and have not been used in real research.

## Question 1.10

What were the two 2024 Nobel Prizes connected to the AlphaGo story, and who received them?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2024: Demis Hassabis (DeepMind CEO) and David Baker for work on protein folding accelerated by AlphaFold.
[*] Nobel Prize in Physics 2024: John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundational work on neural networks that made AlphaGo and AlphaFold possible.
[ ] AlphaGo's creators won the Nobel Prize in Computer Science.
[ ] Lee Sedol received a Nobel Prize for being the only human to beat AlphaGo.
