## Question 5.1

In the "classroom" analogy for self-attention, what do the three cards Q, K, and V represent for a word like "eat"?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Q (Query): what the word is looking for — the question it asks others. For "eat": "Who is doing the eating?"
[*] K (Key): what the word offers — its label that tells others what kind of word it is. For "eat": "I'm an action that needs a subject and object."
[*] V (Value): the actual information the word can share if chosen. For "eat": "eat = action."
[*] Self-attention is a conversation of meaning: every word decides which other words to attend to, guided by how well Q matches K, then gathers information via V.
[ ] Q is the word's output; K is its hidden state; V is its input embedding.
[ ] V (Value) describes what the word is looking for; Q (Query) provides information to others.

## Question 5.2

What are the five steps of the self-attention calculation? Write the final formula.

---

**Answer:**

[*] Step 1: Every word becomes a vector (embedding): x₁, x₂, ... stacked into matrix X.
[*] Step 2: Compute Q = X·W_Q, K = X·W_K, V = X·W_V (learned weight matrices project embeddings into Q/K/V spaces).
[*] Step 3: Compute similarity scores: Scores = Q·Kᵀ (dot product of each Q with every K).
[*] Step 4: Scale by √d_k to prevent large values, then apply softmax to get attention weights (probabilities summing to 1).
[*] Step 5: Weighted sum of values: Output = Attention_Weights × V.
[*] Final formula: Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k) · V
[ ] The formula is: Attention(Q, K, V) = sigmoid(QKᵀ) + V
[ ] Step 3 computes Q + K (sum) rather than Q·Kᵀ (dot product).

## Question 5.3

In the attention weight matrix for "Alice will eat pizza," why does "eat" attend strongly to "Alice" (0.70) but weakly to "will" (0.03)?

---

**Answer:**

[*] After training, the W_Q matrix transforms "eat" into a query vector pointing in the "verb looking for subject" direction.
[*] After training, the W_K matrix transforms "Alice" into a key vector pointing in the "I am a person who can perform actions" direction.
[*] These two directions are aligned — their dot product is large → softmax gives high weight (0.70).
[*] "will" has a key vector pointing in the "auxiliary/modal verb" direction, which does not align well with "eat"'s subject-seeking query → low weight (0.03).
[ ] "eat" attends to "Alice" because the letter 'a' appears in both words.
[ ] The attention weights are predetermined by word frequency in the training data, not learned.

## Question 5.4

What is the difference between training and inference in self-attention? What changes, and what stays the same?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Training: W_Q, W_K, W_V are updated (learned) by backpropagation on billions of sentences. The model adjusts these weights to minimize prediction error.
[*] Inference: W_Q, W_K, W_V are frozen — nothing changes. The model just computes Q = X·W_Q, K = X·W_K, V = X·W_V, then the attention formula.
[*] The 0.70 attention weight for "eat → Alice" is computed on the fly at inference from frozen trained weights — not looked up from memory.
[*] The model learned a skill (general linguistic patterns), not facts (specific sentences). This is why it handles sentences never seen before.
[ ] During inference, the model continues learning from each new sentence the user types.
[ ] W_Q, W_K, W_V are separate for each word — there is one W_Q for "Alice" and a different one for "eat."

## Question 5.5

Why is W_Q a single shared matrix for all words, not a separate matrix per word? How does each word end up with a different query vector?

---

**Answer:**

[*] W_Q is one shared matrix per attention head per layer — it acts as a lens that every word passes through.
[*] Each word gets a different query vector because their input embeddings are different: "Alice" [1.0, 0.5, 0.2, ...] and "eat" [0.2, 0.8, 0.9, ...] produce different results when multiplied by the same W_Q.
[*] W_Q stores a geometric transformation — it rotates and stretches embedding space so that words that should attend to each other end up pointing in similar directions after projection.
[*] Training adjusts W_Q so that verb embeddings × W_Q → "verb looking for subject" direction; subject noun embeddings × W_K → "I am a subject" direction.
[ ] There is a unique W_Q for each word in the vocabulary — training learns one matrix per word.
[ ] W_Q is randomized at inference to prevent the model from memorizing patterns.

## Question 5.6

How large is the "Alice" embedding vector in GPT-2 small? After multiplying by W_Q, how large is the resulting query vector?

---

**Answer:**

[*] "Alice" embedding in GPT-2 small: 768 numbers (d_model = 768).
[*] W_Q shape = d_model × d_k = 768 × 64. After multiplying, q_alice has 64 numbers.
[*] Same for k_alice (64 numbers) and v_alice (64 numbers).
[*] GPT-3 uses d_model = 12,288 → "Alice" embedding = 12,288 numbers; d_k = 128 → query = 128 numbers.
[ ] The query vector is always the same size as the embedding vector — W_Q does not change dimensions.
[ ] In GPT-2 small, "Alice" is represented by just 8 numbers.

## Question 5.7

Why does training require billions of sentences, and what do W_Q and W_K physically encode after training?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Training shows the model pattern after pattern: "Alice will eat ___", "Bob will drink ___", "The dog will chase ___" — each time backpropagation nudges W_Q and W_K slightly toward aligning verb-queries with subject-keys.
[*] After billions of corrections, W_Q encodes the transformation: verb embeddings → "verb looking for subject" direction; noun embeddings → "noun looking for action" direction.
[*] W_K encodes: subject noun embeddings → "I can perform actions" direction; object noun embeddings → "I can be acted upon" direction.
[*] These are geometric transformations stored as matrices of numbers — not rules, not facts, but learned spatial relationships in vector space.
[ ] After training, W_Q and W_K store a lookup table of every subject-verb pair seen in training.
[ ] Training is only needed to adjust V (Value); Q and K are fixed by linguistic theory.

## Question 5.8

What is the training loss function used for next-word prediction, and how does backpropagation use it to update weights?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Loss: L = −log P(correct_word | context). Given "Alice will eat ___", if the model predicts "pizza" with probability p, the loss is −log(p). Lower probability → higher loss.
[*] Backpropagation computes ∂L/∂W_Q using the chain rule and updates: W_Q ← W_Q − η · ∂L/∂W_Q, where η is the learning rate.
[*] This nudges W_Q in the direction that would have produced a higher probability for "pizza" in this context.
[*] Repeated billions of times across many sentences, W_Q, W_K, W_V gradually encode general language structure.
[ ] The loss is computed as the absolute difference between predicted word index and correct word index.
[ ] Backpropagation only updates W_V; W_Q and W_K are updated by a separate algorithm.

## Question 5.10

Why is expensive GPU hardware needed to run inference on LLMs, even though the weights are frozen?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Each inference call requires computing Q = X·W_Q, K = X·W_K, V = X·W_V, then softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)·V — all massive matrix multiplications.
[*] For GPT-3 (175B parameters), these matrix multiplications involve billions of floating-point operations per token generated.
[*] GPUs have thousands of cores designed for parallel matrix math — CPUs would take orders of magnitude longer per token.
[*] The attention weight matrix is n × n where n is the context length — for long contexts, this grows quadratically, requiring significant GPU memory and compute.
[ ] GPUs are only needed for training — inference can be done efficiently on a CPU.
[ ] Inference is cheap because the weights are frozen and nothing needs to be computed.
