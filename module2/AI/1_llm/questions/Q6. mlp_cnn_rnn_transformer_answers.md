# Q6. MLP, CNN, RNN, and Transformer (Answers)

---

## Question 6.1

What is a perceptron, and why is it called the "shared foundation" of MLP, CNN, RNN, and Transformer?

---

**Answer:**

[*] A perceptron is the basic building block: y = f(Σ wᵢxᵢ + b), where inputs xᵢ are multiplied by learned weights wᵢ, summed, and passed through an activation function f (e.g., ReLU or sigmoid).
[*] MLP: stacks perceptrons into layers — perceptrons are the main computation unit.
[*] CNN: applies the same perceptron (called a filter) repeatedly across local regions of the input.
[*] RNN: uses a perceptron (MLP) at each time step, shared across all time steps.
[*] Transformer: uses a 2-layer MLP (FFN) after attention in each layer — at every word position.
[ ] Perceptrons are only used in MLP; CNNs and Transformers replaced them with new mechanisms.
[ ] The perceptron is a Transformer-specific concept introduced in 2017.

---

## Question 6.2

How does CNN differ from MLP in terms of connectivity and weight sharing? Why does this matter for image data?

---

**Answer:**

[*] MLP: every input connects to every neuron — fully connected. A 28×28 image into 512 neurons needs 784 × 512 = 401,408 weights.
[*] CNN: each neuron connects only to a small local window (receptive field). A 3×3 filter needs only 9 weights — reused across all positions (weight sharing). Much fewer parameters.
[*] Weight sharing across space means the same filter detects a pattern (e.g., an edge) anywhere in the image — this is why CNNs are translation invariant.
[*] CNNs stack multiple filters in parallel (64, 128, 512) — each learning a different pattern (edges, corners, textures, shapes).
[ ] CNNs connect every input to every neuron just like MLP, but with a different activation function.
[ ] Weight sharing in CNNs means all layers use exactly the same weights.

## Question 6.3

Explain the convolution operation. What is a "feature map" and what does pooling do?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Convolution: a small filter (e.g., 3×3) with learned weights slides across the input. At each position: z_i = f(Σ w_k · x_{i+k} + b). The same weights are used at every position.
[*] Feature map: the output of applying one filter across the entire input — one value per position, showing where the filter's pattern was detected.
[*] Pooling (e.g., max pooling 2×2): shrinks the feature map by keeping only the strongest activation in each local region — reduces spatial size and makes the representation translation invariant.
[*] Multiple filters in one layer → multiple feature maps. Stacking layers builds increasingly abstract features: edges → shapes → object parts → whole objects.
[ ] A feature map is the input image after color normalization.
[ ] Pooling increases the spatial resolution of the feature map.

## Question 6.4

What is the RNN hidden state, and what is its role as "running memory"?

---

**Answer:**

[*] The hidden state h_t is computed at each step: h_t = f(W_h · h_{t-1} + W_x · x_t + b). It accumulates information from all previously seen words.
[*] h_t acts as running memory — h₁ encodes "I've seen Alice", h₂ encodes "I've seen Alice, will", h₄ encodes everything.
[*] The same weight matrices (W_h, W_x) are reused at every time step — called weight sharing across time.
[*] This is different from Transformer: an RNN must pass information through sequential hidden states, while a Transformer directly attends to all previous words.
[ ] The hidden state is reset to zero at the start of every new word.
[ ] In an RNN, W_h is a separate matrix for each time step — not shared.

## Question 6.5

What is the vanishing gradient problem in RNNs, and how does it relate to word distance?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Backpropagation through time: gradients flow backward through each step — multiplied by W_h and the activation's derivative at each step.
[*] With sigmoid-like activations, each multiplication shrinks the gradient: after many steps, the gradient reaching early layers is essentially zero.
[*] Formula: h₄ ≈ f(W_h · f(W_h · f(W_h · h₁))) — the signal from h₁ (Alice) fades through multiple nonlinear functions by the time we process later words.
[*] Result: early words in a long sequence have little influence on the model's predictions at the end — making long-range dependencies hard to learn.
[ ] The vanishing gradient problem means the RNN ignores the most recent words in the sequence.
[ ] LSTM completely eliminates the vanishing gradient problem.

## Question 6.6

What are the two components of one Transformer layer, and what role does each play?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Self-Attention: computes relationships between all word pairs simultaneously — answers "which words matter to me?" and aggregates context from relevant words.
[*] Feed-Forward Network (FFN): a 2-layer MLP applied independently to each word position — FFN(x) = ReLU(x·W₁ + b₁)·W₂ + b₂. Answers "what do I do with the information I gathered?"
[*] Research shows the FFN is where factual knowledge is stored — "Paris is the capital of France" lives in the FFN weights, not in the attention weights.
[*] Removing the FFN from a Transformer significantly degrades performance — attention alone is not sufficient.
[ ] The Transformer replaced perceptrons (MLP) entirely with self-attention — no FFN is used.
[ ] The FFN handles word ordering; attention handles word meaning.

## Question 6.7

Compare RNN and Transformer side by side across four dimensions: parallelism, long-range dependencies, word ordering, and scalability.

---

**Answer:**

[*] Parallelism: RNN = sequential (each step depends on previous), cannot be parallelized during training. Transformer = all words processed simultaneously, highly parallelizable on GPUs.
[*] Long-range dependencies: RNN = signal fades over many steps (vanishing gradient). Transformer = any word directly attends to any other word with a single operation.
[*] Word ordering: RNN = order is implicit in the sequential processing. Transformer = requires explicit positional encoding (no inherent sense of order).
[*] Scalability: RNN = performance degrades on long sentences; Transformer = scales smoothly with more data, parameters, and compute — enabled GPT-3, GPT-4, etc.
[ ] RNNs scale better than Transformers for very long sequences.
[ ] Transformers process words sequentially but use a more efficient hidden state than RNNs.

## Question 6.8

What is the "evolution, not revolution" timeline from 1943 to 2018+? Name at least 6 key steps.

---

**Answer:**

[*] 1943: Perceptron (McCulloch & Pitts) — first artificial neuron.
[*] 1980s: Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) — stacked perceptrons.
[*] 1986: Backpropagation (Rumelhart et al.) — enables training deep networks.
[*] 1989: CNN — MLP + local filters + weight sharing (LeCun et al.).
[*] 1990s: RNN — MLP + hidden state across time.
[*] 1997: LSTM — RNN + gating mechanism to address vanishing gradients.
[*] 2012: AlexNet — CNN dominates image recognition.
[*] 2017: Transformer — Attention + MLP, no recurrence.
[*] 2018+: BERT, GPT, Claude, LLaMA — scaled Transformers.
[ ] CNN was invented in 2017 as part of the Transformer paper.
[ ] The Transformer replaced all previous architectures and none of their ideas are used today.

## Question 6.9

Why did the Transformer win over CNNs and RNNs? What three key advantages did it have?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Parallelism: RNNs could not efficiently use GPU hardware because each step depends on the previous. Transformers process everything at once — perfectly suited for GPU parallel cores.
[*] Long-range dependencies: a Transformer connects word 1 to word 500 with one attention operation. An RNN must pass the signal through 499 sequential states — the signal fades.
[*] Scalability: Transformer performance follows smooth scaling laws — performance improves predictably with more data, more parameters, and more compute. This enabled GPT-3 (175B), GPT-4, and beyond.
[ ] Transformers won because they required much less training data than RNNs.
[ ] CNNs and RNNs were fundamentally flawed — Transformers fixed their conceptual errors.

## Question 6.10

(T/F) The lecture states that "Transformers replaced perceptrons with attention" is a misconception — in fact, Transformers use both attention AND perceptrons (FFN), and removing the FFN significantly degrades performance.

---

**Answer:**

*a) True
b) False
... The lecture explicitly corrects this: "A common misconception: 'Transformers replaced perceptrons with attention.' This is wrong. Attention is not a perceptron — it has no activation function and no learned bias. The perceptron (FFN) is still essential. Attention handles relationships between words (relational reasoning); FFN handles transformation of each word's representation (local processing). The Transformer is attention + perceptrons, not attention instead of perceptrons."
