## Question 2.1

What is the "DHA" framework, and why does the lecture use it to explain the history of deep learning?

---

**Answer:**

[*] DHA stands for Data, Hardware, and Algorithms — the three factors whose convergence made deep learning possible.
[*] The lecture uses it because each historical breakthrough can be understood as a change in one or more of these three factors.
[*] Remove any one of the three and deep learning would not have succeeded: good algorithms on bad hardware with little data fails; great data with bad algorithms fails; etc.
[ ] DHA stands for Deep, Hierarchical, and Adaptive — describing the properties of neural networks.
[ ] The framework explains why parallel communication is inferior to serial communication.

## Question 2.2

What is a perceptron, and how does it learn? What major limitation did Minsky and Papert prove in 1969?

---

**Answer:**

[*] A perceptron is the simplest neural network — a single neuron that takes inputs, multiplies each by a weight, sums them, and outputs 1 if the result exceeds a threshold, else 0.
[*] It learns by adjusting weights from labeled examples (data-driven learning).
[*] Minsky and Papert proved mathematically that a single perceptron cannot learn XOR — XOR is not linearly separable (you need more than a straight line to separate the classes).
[*] Their book implied multi-layer networks would be equally hopeless, causing the first AI winter — funding dried up and researchers abandoned the field.
[ ] Minsky proved that perceptrons could never learn any function, not just XOR.
[ ] The first AI winter was caused by a hardware shortage, not a theoretical result.

## Question 2.3

What is the "credit assignment problem," and how does backpropagation solve it?

---

**Answer:**

[*] The credit assignment problem: when a multi-layer network makes a wrong prediction, which of the hundreds of weights across all layers are responsible for the error?
[*] Backpropagation solves it using the chain rule from calculus — it propagates the error backwards through the network layer by layer, computing how much each weight contributed.
[*] The factory assembly line analogy: trace backwards from the defective car through Assembly → Part B → Part A, measuring how much each stage contributed to the defect.
[*] Hinton, Rumelhart, and Williams popularized this in 1986, enabling training of multi-layer networks.
[ ] Backpropagation sends the error forward through the network to find faulty weights.
[ ] The credit assignment problem only applies to networks with more than 100 layers.

## Question 2.4

What is the vanishing gradient problem, and how does ReLU solve it?

---

**Answer:**

[*] The vanishing gradient problem: the sigmoid activation function has a maximum gradient of only 0.25. During backpropagation, gradients are multiplied layer by layer — for 10 layers: 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 0.00000095. Early layers receive nearly zero gradient and cannot learn.
[*] ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit): f(x) = max(0, x). Its gradient is either 0 or 1 — gradients don't shrink as they flow back through ReLU layers.
[*] Replacing sigmoid with ReLU in 2010–2012 was one of the key changes that unlocked deep learning.
[ ] ReLU solves the vanishing gradient problem by amplifying all gradients to be larger than 1.
[ ] The vanishing gradient problem only occurs with more than 1,000 layers.

## Question 2.5

What made AlexNet's 2012 ImageNet result so dramatic? What were the error rates before and after?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Best traditional methods: 28.2% error (2010), 25.8% error (2011) — slow annual improvement.
[*] AlexNet (2012): 16.4% error — a jump of nearly 10 percentage points, vs second-place's 26.2%.
[*] The lecture calls it "an earthquake" — the entire computer vision community abandoned traditional methods and switched to deep learning.
[*] By 2015, ResNet achieved 3.6% error, surpassing human-level performance (~5%) on ImageNet.
[ ] AlexNet's improvement was modest — only 1–2 percentage points better than the previous year.
[ ] AlexNet was the first neural network ever applied to image classification.

## Question 2.6

What four ingredients made AlexNet work? Why is none of them individually the reason?

---

**Answer:**

[*] ReLU activation — solved the vanishing gradient problem.
[*] GPU training — trained on two NVIDIA GTX 580 GPUs, a first for competition-scale deep learning.
[*] ImageNet data — 1.2 million labeled images provided enough data to learn from.
[*] Dropout — prevented overfitting by randomly disabling neurons during training.
[*] No single idea was new — but combining them at scale produced a result nobody expected. This is a recurring theme: old ideas + more data + better hardware = breakthroughs.
[ ] AlexNet worked because of a brand-new algorithm invented specifically for it.
[ ] GPU training alone was sufficient to explain AlexNet's victory.

## Question 2.7

What is the residual connection (ResNet), and what problem did it solve? Why is it still important today?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Problem: a 56-layer network performed worse than a 20-layer network (not due to overfitting — training error was also worse). Deeper networks were harder to optimize.
[*] Residual connection: H(x) = x + F(x). The layer learns only the "difference" F(x) from the input. If a layer has nothing useful to learn, F(x) = 0 and the signal passes through unchanged — the network can never get worse by adding more layers.
[*] ResNet won the 2015 ImageNet challenge with 3.6% error, surpassing human performance.
[*] Residual connections are used in every modern LLM (GPT, Claude, LLaMA) and every modern image model — one of the most important ideas in all of deep learning.
[ ] Residual connections increase the depth of the network by adding more layers automatically.
[ ] ResNet was important only for image classification and is not used in language models.

## Question 2.8

What is Word2Vec, and what famous equation did it produce?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Word2Vec (Mikolov, 2013): a method to learn dense vector representations of words by training a model to predict a word from its neighbors. Words with similar meanings end up close together in vector space.
[*] Training idea: given "The ___ sat on the throne", the model learns that "king" and "queen" both fit, so they get similar vectors.
[*] Famous result: vec(king) − vec(man) + vec(woman) ≈ vec(queen). The model learned relationship analogies purely from text.
[*] Other examples: vec(Paris) − vec(France) + vec(Japan) ≈ vec(Tokyo). Nobody programmed these relationships — they emerged from patterns in data.
[ ] Word2Vec was trained using one-hot encoding, which already captures word relationships.
[ ] The king–queen equation was manually programmed by researchers to demonstrate the system.

## Question 2.9

What was the key problem with RNNs/LSTMs that the Transformer solved?

---

**Answer:**

[*] RNNs/LSTMs processed text one word at a time: each step depends on the previous step's hidden state, so they cannot be parallelized.
[*] They were "forgetful": by the end of a long sentence, information about the beginning had faded through multiple nonlinear transformations.
[*] Transformers process all words simultaneously using self-attention — every word can directly attend to every other word in the input.
[*] Transformers are massively parallel (perfect for GPUs) and handle long-range dependencies easily.
[ ] RNNs were too fast for GPUs — Transformers slowed them down to use hardware more efficiently.
[ ] LSTMs were replaced because they required too much labeled training data.

## Question 2.10

What is "The Bitter Lesson" (Sutton, 2019), and what pattern does it describe in AI history?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Richard Sutton's argument: the biggest lesson from 70 years of AI research is that general methods leveraging computation are ultimately the most effective — by a large margin.
[*] Researchers repeatedly built clever, hand-crafted systems encoding human knowledge (expert systems, rule-based NLP, feature engineering) and were repeatedly beaten by simple methods + more compute + more data.
[*] Examples: hand-crafted chess evaluation lost to neural nets; hand-crafted vision features (SIFT, HOG) lost to CNNs; rule-based NLP lost to LLMs that just predict the next word.
[*] It is "bitter" because researchers spend years building elegant domain-specific solutions, then a scaled-up simple approach beats them and makes all that expertise obsolete.
[ ] The Bitter Lesson says that clever algorithms always beat brute-force approaches.
[ ] The Bitter Lesson was written to encourage researchers to build more domain-specific AI systems.

What warning did Geoffrey Hinton give after leaving Google in 2023?

---

**Answer:**

[*] Hinton resigned from Google in 2023 to speak freely about the dangers of AI.
[*] He said he now partly regrets his life's work — and that AI systems could become smarter than humans much sooner than he previously thought.
[*] He had previously believed general AI surpassing humans was 30–50 years away; he no longer believes that.
[*] The lecture notes: "The Godfather who spent his life convincing the world that neural networks could work is now warning the world that they work too well."
[ ] Hinton said AI is safe and there is nothing to worry about.
[ ] Hinton left Google because he was dissatisfied with his research results.
